Before you can answer "which film camera should I buy," there's a more
useful question to answer first: which *type*. SLR, rangefinder, TLR, and
point-and-shoot aren't just different bodies — they're different ways of
seeing and composing a shot, and that difference matters more to how you'll
actually enjoy shooting than any single model's spec sheet.

## Why "which type" beats "which specific model"

New shooters tend to start by researching individual cameras — the Pentax
K1000, the Canon AE-1, a Yashica TLR — without first deciding what kind of
shooting experience they actually want. That's backwards. Two SLRs from
different brands are more similar to each other than an SLR is to a
rangefinder, because the type of camera determines how you view, focus, and
compose — the model mostly determines build quality, lens ecosystem, and
price. Pick the type first, and the specific-model research gets much
easier.

## SLR (single-lens reflex)

An SLR uses a single lens for both viewing and shooting. A mirror sits
behind the lens at an angle, bouncing the image up through a pentaprism (or
pentamirror) and into the viewfinder — so what you see is, almost exactly,
what the lens sees. Press the shutter and the mirror flips up out of the way
for the split second the film is exposed, then drops back down.

That mirror mechanism is the whole tradeoff in one part: it gives you true
through-the-lens framing and focusing, at the cost of size, weight, a
distinct "clack" of a sound, and a brief viewfinder blackout at the moment
of exposure. It's also the most flexible type for interchangeable lenses,
since the mirror box and prism don't care what focal length is mounted in
front of them.

My own body, the Pentax KM (covered in more detail in [my full review](/journal/pentax-km-review)),
is an SLR — SMC Pentax-M lenses mount directly, and the depth-of-field
preview lever is a feature that only really makes sense on this type of
camera.

In practice, the shutter sound is what I notice most. That mechanical clack
is oddly satisfying after shooting motorized point-and-shoots or even my
digital Lumix GX85 — though it does mean I can't shoot quietly in a hushed
space the way a rangefinder lets you. The mirror blackout barely registers
to me anymore; it's a fraction of a second, and I've never actually lost a
moment to it. What I do dislike is the weight over a full day out — the KM
with the 50mm f/1.4 attached gets noticeably heavier in the hand after a few
hours walking around Singapore than a pancake-lens setup does, which is
exactly why the 40mm f/2.8 pancake is what I reach for on longer days
rather than the faster lens.

## Rangefinder

A rangefinder doesn't look through the lens at all. Instead, it uses a
separate viewfinder window and a coincident-image focusing mechanism — two
faint overlapping images that you align by turning the focus ring, and which
snap into one sharp image when you've nailed focus. No mirror, no prism, no
mirror slap.

That absence is the whole appeal: rangefinders are typically lighter, more
compact, and much quieter than an equivalent SLR, since there's no mirror
mechanism to move. The tradeoffs are real, though. You can't see depth of
field through the viewfinder the way an SLR lets you (no true through-the-
lens preview), focusing takes practice to get fast at, and at very close
distances the viewfinder's slightly offset position from the lens
introduces parallax error — what you framed isn't quite what the lens
captured.

Rangefinders earn their reputation with street and travel photographers
specifically because of the quiet shutter and compact size — a rangefinder
can be genuinely unobtrusive in a way a clacking SLR isn't.

## TLR (twin-lens reflex)

A TLR uses two lenses stacked vertically: a viewing lens on top, feeding a
ground-glass focusing screen you look down into, and a separate taking lens
below it that actually exposes the film. You compose and focus by holding
the camera at waist level and looking down, rather than up to your eye.

Two things make TLRs distinctive. First, the waist-level view genuinely
changes how you shoot and compose — it's a slower, more deliberate way of
working, and the image in the finder is mirror-flipped left-to-right, which
takes some adjustment. Second, almost all TLRs shoot medium format (typically
6×6cm square negatives on 120 film), which is a separate, bigger topic than
camera type — see the aside below. The twin-lens design also means the same
parallax issue as a rangefinder, usually more pronounced at close focusing
distances, since the viewing and taking lenses are physically further apart
vertically than a rangefinder's viewfinder and lens are horizontally.

## Point-and-shoot / compact

Point-and-shoots handle exposure and focus automatically, in a small, often
pocketable body with a fixed or modest zoom lens. What you give up is
control — no manual exposure on most models, a lens you can't swap, often no
real depth-of-field control — in exchange for genuine portability and speed.
Their current resurgence isn't nostalgia alone: for candid, spontaneous
shooting where you don't want to think about settings, a good point-and-
shoot still does something an SLR or rangefinder can't do as easily — get
out of your own way.

## A quick clarifying aside: format size vs. camera type

It's easy to conflate "TLR" with "medium format" since almost all TLRs
shoot 120 film, but they're actually two separate ideas. Camera type
(SLR, rangefinder, TLR, point-and-shoot) describes how you view and focus.
Film format (35mm vs. medium format vs. large format) describes the size of
the negative itself, which affects resolution, grain, and depth of field
independently of camera type — there are 35mm rangefinders and medium-format
SLRs both. If you're shopping and a listing says "TLR," assume medium format
unless stated otherwise, but don't assume the reverse.

## Decision guide: matching camera type to shooting style

- **Street or travel photography**, where size and discretion matter: a
  rangefinder or a genuinely compact point-and-shoot.
- **Learning manual exposure and wanting to see exactly what the lens sees**:
  an SLR — the through-the-lens view removes a layer of guesswork while
  you're still building intuition.
- **Portraits, still life, or a slower, more deliberate process**: a TLR's
  waist-level workflow forces a different pace that some photographers find
  genuinely improves their compositions.
- **Fast, candid, low-effort shooting**: a point-and-shoot, accepting the
  loss of manual control as the price of speed.

## Which type Andrea recommends starting with

I'd tell a total beginner to start with an SLR, and specifically one like
the KM. Seeing exactly what the lens sees while you're still learning
aperture and depth of field removes one layer of guesswork that a
rangefinder's offset viewfinder adds — you're not translating between two
different views while also learning exposure from scratch. I'd point people
toward a rangefinder as a deliberate second camera once manual exposure is
second nature, not as a starting point, no matter how appealing the compact
size looks in photos.
