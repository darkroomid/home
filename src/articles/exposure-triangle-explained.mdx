The exposure triangle isn't a film-specific concept, but film is where it
actually bites. There's no LCD to check your result, no free reshoot if
you guessed wrong, and — the part that trips people up coming from digital —
your ISO is locked for the entire roll, not adjustable frame to frame. Get
comfortable with how aperture, shutter speed, and ISO trade off against each
other, and a roll of film stops feeling like a gamble.

## Why this matters more on film than digital

On a digital body, a bad guess costs you a glance at the histogram and a
retake. On film, a bad guess costs you a frame you won't see until the roll
is developed — sometimes weeks later. And because ISO is fixed by whichever
roll is loaded, you only have two variables to actually adjust in the
moment: aperture and shutter speed. That constraint is exactly why
understanding the triangle properly matters more here, not less — you're
working with fewer knobs, so you need to actually understand what each one
does rather than lean on a live preview to bail you out.

## Aperture: light and depth of field

Aperture is the opening in the lens that controls how much light reaches
the film, measured in f-stops — counterintuitively, a smaller f-number
(f/1.4) means a wider opening and more light, while a larger f-number
(f/16) means a smaller opening and less light. The same setting also
controls depth of field: wide open, only a thin slice of the frame is in
sharp focus; stopped down, much more of the scene reads sharp front to
back.

A practical way to build intuition: f/1.4 to f/2.8 is "isolate the
subject," f/5.6 to f/8 is "general use, forgiving depth of field," and f/11
to f/16 is "everything in focus, landscape territory." I made that call
directly on the hero image above — I shot the National Gallery Singapore's
facade at f/5.6 specifically because I wanted the architectural detail
sharp across most of the frame without needing to stop all the way down to
f/16 and lose the shutter speed I'd have needed to hand-hold steadily in
that light.

## Shutter speed: light and motion

Shutter speed controls how long the film is exposed, and like aperture, it
also has a second job: freezing or blurring motion. A fast shutter (1/500s
and up) freezes movement; a slow one (1/30s and below) lets motion blur
into the frame, whether that's a deliberate creative choice or just camera
shake you didn't want.

For handheld shooting without stabilization, a practical floor — tighter
than the textbook reciprocal rule of "shutter speed roughly matching your
focal length" — is to not trust yourself below about 1/60s with a normal
lens unless you're deliberately bracing against something solid. Below
that, the odds of visible shake creeping into an otherwise well-composed
frame go up fast.

## ISO: fixed per roll, and why that matters

ISO measures the film's sensitivity to light — higher ISO needs less light
to expose correctly, at the cost of more visible grain. On digital, you can
change ISO frame to frame to compensate for changing light. On film, ISO is
baked into the roll you loaded, so it's the one corner of the triangle you
commit to before you start shooting, not adjust in the moment.

That single fixed variable changes how you think about the other two.
Instead of asking "what ISO do I need for this light," you're asking "given
the ISO I already committed to, what aperture and shutter speed combination
gets me a correct exposure right now" — a subtly different, more
constrained problem that rewards understanding the trade-off between the
other two corners rather than reaching for a third dial.

## How the three interact: one worked example

Say you're shooting ISO 200 film in daylight bright enough for a baseline
exposure of f/8 at 1/250s. That's one correct exposure — but not the only
one. Move to f/5.6 (one stop more light from the wider aperture) and you
need to compensate with 1/500s (one stop less light from the faster
shutter) to land on the same total exposure, now with shallower depth of
field. Move instead to f/11 (one stop less light) and you'd need 1/125s
(one stop more light) to compensate, now with deeper depth of field and a
slightly higher motion-blur risk if your subject's moving.

All three of those combinations expose the film identically. What changes
is what the photo actually looks like — how much is in focus, and how
frozen or blurred any motion is. That's the real lesson of the triangle:
it's not really about getting "a" correct exposure, it's about choosing
*which* correct exposure, out of several available, actually serves the
shot you're trying to make.

## Common beginner mistakes

- **Chasing "correct" exposure without deciding what you actually want.**
  Any of several aperture/shutter combinations can be technically correct —
  the mistake is defaulting to whichever one your meter suggests first
  instead of asking what depth of field or motion treatment the shot needs.
- **Forgetting your loaded ISO mid-roll.** Easy to do if you shoot multiple
  bodies loaded with different stocks — metering as if you're still on the
  roll you had in a different camera last week.
- **Treating shutter speed purely as an exposure tool and ignoring motion.**
  A "correctly exposed" frame can still be ruined by unintended blur if you
  picked a slow shutter speed purely to compensate for a small aperture,
  without checking whether your subject (or your hands) could hold still
  for it.

## A simple mental framework for the field

When you don't have time to do the math: decide what matters most for the
specific shot — depth of field or motion — and let that decide your
starting point. If depth of field matters most (a portrait, a detail shot),
pick your aperture first and let shutter speed follow to match the light.
If motion matters most (something moving, low light where camera shake is
the risk), pick your shutter speed first and let aperture follow instead.
Either way, you're only ever solving for one remaining variable at a time,
with your ISO already fixed for the roll — which is a much faster decision
than trying to juggle all three at once.
